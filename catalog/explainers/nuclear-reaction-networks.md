## Introduction

This entry is not an artificial chemistry that someone invented. It is real
nuclear physics, written down as a reaction network. In the nuclear reactions
that power stars and that made the light elements after the Big Bang, atomic
nuclei collide and turn into other nuclei, releasing particles and energy. That
is formally the same kind of object as a chemical reaction network: the
"molecules" are nuclei and elementary particles, and the "reactions" are
fusions and decays. Banzhaf and Yamamoto make this point in their chapter
"Beyond Chemistry and Biology" (book §20.2), where they call nuclear and
particle physics "primary examples of reaction systems that could be
formulated from an AC point of view". As in chemistry, they note, collisions of
two partners are much more likely than collisions of three or more, and every
reaction must respect conservation laws such as those for charge and energy.

The section's central example is the **CNO cycle**, also called the
Bethe-Weizsäcker cycle, which according to the book "dominates as a reaction
pathway in stars with larger masses than that of the sun". Carbon-12 captures a
proton, becomes nitrogen, then oxygen, and after six steps falls apart into
helium-4 and carbon-12 again. The carbon, nitrogen and oxygen nuclei are
catalysts: they are used up and regenerated on every turn, while hydrogen is
burnt to helium. The book uses the cycle as an instance of an *organisation*,
the idea from its chapter 12 of a set of species that is **closed** (reactions
among its members produce nothing outside the set) and **self-maintaining**
(every member is regenerated by reactions among the members). The cycle is
open, as the book stresses: hydrogen flows in, helium, positrons and neutrinos
flow out.

The book's second example is **big-bang nucleosynthesis**: the network of
reactions that, in the first minutes of the universe, turned primordial protons
and neutrons into deuterium, helium and lithium, "leading to the observed
abundances of light elements in the universe". Its figure 20.4 draws that
network, adapted from Coc et al. (2012).

So this is a set of *written-down networks*, not a simulation model with its
own dynamics. Chemart gives three of them: the CNO cycle, the pp-I branch of
the proton-proton (pp) chain, another way of burning hydrogen to helium, and a
12-reaction core of big-bang nucleosynthesis. Its closest neighbours in the
catalog are the other chapter-20 entries that carry the AC view outside
chemistry, [mechanical self-assembly](mechanical-self-assembly.md),
[SOAS](soas.md), the [N-economy](n-economy.md) and the
[AC of social communication](social-communication-ac.md). What sets this one
apart, from them and from most of the catalog, is that its reactions are not a
model someone designed but real nuclear processes, whose rates have been
measured or calculated in the physics literature. For the
organisation concept it illustrates, see
[computing with chemical organizations](organization-computing.md).

## How it works

### Species and their names

A species is a nucleus or a particle. Nuclei are written as mass number
followed by element symbol: `1H` is a proton (a hydrogen nucleus), `2H`
deuterium, `3H` tritium, `3He` and `4He` the two stable helium nuclei, `12C`
carbon-12, and so on. The mass number is the number of nucleons (protons plus
neutrons). The particles are `n` (neutron), `e+` (positron), `e-` (electron),
`nu_e` (electron neutrino), `anti_nu_e` (electron antineutrino) and `gamma`
(a photon). Nuclei are treated as fully ionised, that is without their
electrons.

A reaction is a fusion (two nuclei, or a nucleus and a neutron, become one) or
a decay (one nucleus becomes another, emitting particles). Every reaction has
one or two reactants, as in the book.

### Worked example: the CNO cycle

The default network is book equation 20.4, the six reactions printed below
under *Using it in Chemart*. Follow one carbon nucleus around:

```
12C + 1H -> 13N + gamma      carbon-12 captures a proton, becomes nitrogen-13
13N -> 13C + e+ + nu_e       nitrogen-13 decays to carbon-13
13C + 1H -> 14N + gamma      a second proton capture
14N + 1H -> 15O + gamma      a third
15O -> 15N + e+ + nu_e       oxygen-15 decays to nitrogen-15
15N + 1H -> 12C + 4He        a fourth proton splits it into carbon-12 and helium-4
```

Adding up all six reactions, each fired once, gives the net effect of one turn.
Running that sum on the generated network (code under *Using it*) gives

```
{'1H': -4, 'gamma': 3, 'e+': 2, 'nu_e': 2, '4He': 1}
```

that is `4 1H -> 4He + 2 e+ + 2 nu_e + 3 gamma`. The six species `12C`, `13N`,
`13C`, `14N`, `15O` and `15N` have net change zero: they are the catalysts,
and they vanish from the overall reaction even though each appears in two of
the six steps.

This is also why the set is an organisation in the book's sense. Every species
the cycle produces is in the set (closure), and every catalyst is regenerated
by one of the six reactions (self-maintenance), provided hydrogen is supplied
from outside. Chemart does not model the inflow and outflow as reactions; it
records them in `net.extras["boundary"]`.

### Conservation laws

Nuclear reactions obey exact conservation laws, and Chemart attaches three of
them to every network in `net.extras["conservation"]`, as a weight per species:

- **baryon number**: the number of nucleons, equal to the mass number of a
  nucleus (1 for `n` and `1H`, 12 for `12C`, 0 for the light particles);
- **charge**: the number of protons of a fully ionised nucleus, +1 for `e+`,
  -1 for `e-`;
- **electron lepton number**: +1 for `e-` and `nu_e`, -1 for `e+` and
  `anti_nu_e`.

In `13N -> 13C + e+ + nu_e`, for example, baryon number is 13 on both sides,
charge is 7 = 6 + 1, and lepton number is 0 = -1 + 1. The conserved "mass" in
the at-a-glance table is this baryon number (the nucleon count), not rest mass,
which these energy-releasing reactions do not conserve exactly.

### What is not here: rates

The formal specification below names an ODE reactor (deterministic rate
equations), but Chemart does not supply rates. Nuclear reaction rates depend steeply on temperature and density
through measured or calculated cross sections, which the book does not give,
so every reaction here has no rate constant and the network cannot be
simulated as it stands. What you get is the topology, the stoichiometry and
the conservation laws.

## Using it

The default run is the CNO cycle of book equation 20.4, with its open boundary
recorded as

```python
net.extras["boundary"]    # {'inflow': ['1H'], 'outflow': ['4He', 'e+', 'nu_e']}
```

and three conservation laws in `net.extras["conservation"]`, each a
`{"name": ..., "vector": {species: weight}}` dictionary. The `seed` has no
effect: all three networks are fixed lists.

The net reaction of the cycle, and a check of the conservation laws, take a
few lines with the stoichiometric matrices:

```python
import numpy as np, chemart
net = chemart.generate_network("nuclear-reaction-networks", seed=1)
ids, R, P = net.matrices()
S = (P - R).toarray()                        # species x reactions
net_change = S @ np.ones(len(net.reactions))  # every reaction fires once
print({s: int(x) for s, x in zip(ids, net_change) if x})
for law in net.extras["conservation"]:
    m = np.array([law["vector"].get(s, 0) for s in ids])
    print(law["name"], (m @ S).tolist())
```

```
{'1H': -4, 'gamma': 3, 'e+': 2, 'nu_e': 2, '4He': 1}
baryon number [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]
charge [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]
electron lepton number [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]
```

**The pp chain.** `network="pp-chain"` gives book equation 20.3 (two protons
fuse to deuterium) completed with the two standard steps of the pp-I branch:

```
2 1H -> 2H + e+ + nu_e
2H + 1H -> 3He + gamma
2 3He -> 4He + 2 1H
```

Read `2 1H` as "two protons" and `2 3He` as "two helium-3 nuclei": the leading
number is the stoichiometric coefficient. Firing the three reactions 2, 2 and
1 times gives the same overall burning as the CNO cycle, but with no
catalyst: `{'1H': -4, 'e+': 2, 'nu_e': 2, 'gamma': 2, '4He': 1}`. There is no
boundary entry for this network.

**Big-bang nucleosynthesis.** `network="big-bang-nucleosynthesis"` gives 11
species and 12 reactions among the neutron, protons and the light nuclei up to
lithium and beryllium-7:

```
n -> 1H + e- + anti_nu_e
1H + n -> 2H + gamma
2H + 1H -> 3He + gamma
2 2H -> 3He + n
2 2H -> 3H + 1H
3H + 2H -> 4He + n
3He + n -> 3H + 1H
3He + 2H -> 4He + 1H
3He + 4He -> 7Be + gamma
3H + 4He -> 7Li + gamma
7Be + n -> 7Li + 1H
7Li + 1H -> 2 4He
```

The book's figure 20.4 only draws the network, without listing its reactions,
so this is a standard core network chosen in its place (see the implementation
decisions). It has neither reverse reactions nor rates.

## Results

The book presents nuclear physics as an illustration, not as a model with its
own results, and Chemart's networks carry no dynamics. What there is to report
is the two phenomena the catalog lists, and the published work the book points
to.

**The CNO cycle as an organisation.** The book's claim is structural: the six
reactions form a closed, self-maintaining set with carbon, nitrogen and oxygen
as catalysts, fed by hydrogen. Chemart reproduces the structure exactly. Its
tests (`tests/chemistries/test_w1_structure.py`) check that the default network
is book equation 20.4 reaction for reaction, and that baryon number, charge
and lepton number are conserved by every reaction of all three networks. The
tests do not check closure or self-maintenance as such, and Chemart has no
organisation-analysis tool; the net-reaction calculation above shows the
catalysts cancelling.

**Light-element abundances from big-bang nucleosynthesis.** The book cites Coc
et al. (2012) for its figure. That paper computes the primordial abundances
with an extended network of 59 nuclides, from the neutron to sodium-23, linked
by 424 reactions (391 nuclear reactions plus 33 beta decays), each
supplemented by its reverse reaction, with about 270 rates calculated with the
TALYS nuclear-reaction code. At the baryon density measured by the WMAP
satellite (seven-year data) it finds a helium-4 mass fraction of 0.2476,
D/H = 2.59 × 10⁻⁵, ³He/H = 1.04 × 10⁻⁵ and ⁷Li/H = 5.24 × 10⁻¹⁰, against
observed values of 0.2561 ± 0.0108, (2.82 ± 0.2) × 10⁻⁵, (1.1 ± 0.2) × 10⁻⁵
and (1.58 ± 0.31) × 10⁻¹⁰ (their Table 2). Deuterium agrees, helium fairly
well given the observational uncertainty, but lithium comes out 3 to 4 times
too high, the "lithium problem" that the larger network does not remove. A
13-reaction network gives the light-element abundances to within about 1–2% of
the 424-reaction one. The extension matters for heavier traces: the paper
finds a primordial CNO/H ratio close to 0.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ and raises the beryllium-9
estimate by a factor of 4.

Chemart does **not** reproduce any of these abundances. Computing them needs
temperature-dependent rates and the expansion history of the early universe,
neither of which is in the book or in Chemart. Chemart's 12-reaction network
is a structural stand-in for figure 20.4; it is not the paper's 13-reaction
network nor its 424-reaction one.

**Also in the section, not implemented.** The book gives one particle-physics
reaction, `d + nu_e -> p + n + nu_e` (equation 20.5, a neutrino breaking up a
deuteron), and closes with the remark that "at the lowest level, a network of
reactions connects all matter". Chemart has no particle-physics network.
